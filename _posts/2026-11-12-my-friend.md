In Oxford, I dined often with Prince Harry. We made for a good match. Study abroad is a lonely affair, and by some irony, the Duke of Sussex understood as well as I did how it feels to stand alone on the outskirts of British society. 

It’s not that I didn’t have friends. And it’s not that Oxford is an unfriendly place. It’s just that Oxford, in all its pomp and circumstance, its matriculation gowns and port-that-must-always-be-passed-to-the-left and so many hidden doors, makes itself a very difficult place to study abroad in. 

***

On paper, the experience had looked idyllic. I enrolled as a visiting student at St. Catherine’s College, which, founded in 1962, takes great pride in being the most forward-looking of the residential colleges. Danish architect Arne Jacobsen designed every detail, from the choice of fish in ponds to the shape of the butter knives, and rumor has it that he was rather depressed while doing so. The effect: one feels more that they are living in a Scandinavian prison than the gothic castles that Oxford usually brings to mind. Jacobsen likewise did away with any kind of communal spaces in the dorms, which meant that my contact with the neighbors was limited to the soundtrack of their getting out of bed while I made coffee in my room. 

Classes, then, to integrate myself into Oxonian life! But alas, the University takes pride in having done away with these as well. Instead, I had tutorials: hour-long encounters between me and a professor where we discussed my weekly essays on Medieval French literature and French generative syntax. A fine way to learn (and these did, at least, scratch the itch of exposure to classic Oxonian architecture); less so as a way to meet any peers.

By a stroke of pure luck, I did manage to infiltrate a French translation workshop, which furnished a blissful hour of polyphony on Monday evenings. My imposition on the group’s dinner table the first week gave way to a standing Monday night trivia invitation, where a Hollie and a Laura introduced me to a Charlotte and a Tom and a Ben. All were quite lovely, and all enjoyed talking at length about movies I hadn’t seen and places I hadn’t been using Britishisms I couldn’t understand. And thus were my evenings. I’d sit in the Junior Common Room following the contours of their conversations until the lilting up-and-down of the accents made me seasick, then I’d pour out the second half of my pint and go to bed. It’s not that Oxford was unfriendly. It’s just that I felt like an eternal tourist, a real Oxford life felt perpetually out of reach. 

***

One drizzly spring Saturday, with the weekend stretching blankly before me and the silence teetering between oppressive and unbearable, I took a desperate stand. I opened my phone’s audiobook app, typed in “England,” and clicked on the first thing that came up: Prince Harry’s memoir Spare. The name meant almost nothing to me. It took a Google search to place him as the second son of Princess Diana and King Charles, grandson to the late Queen Elizabeth and younger brother to Prince William, heir to the throne. And it took sixteen hours of his own narration for me to learn that most of England sees him as a deplorable royal thickhead: whiny, uncouth, and military-obsessed; defined by a troubled adolescence, that pesky Meghan Markle, and his ongoing wars with the press. To me, he was just a sullen redhead staring up from the screen with stormy eyes. It looked like it would match the mood, so I zipped up my raincoat, put in my airpods, and started to walk. 

***

His voice. It had me from his very first words. The humanity. The way each round vowel melted into a pool of melancholy, and the heaviness that hung over every syllable, the depth and bitterness and those occasional flickers of warmth. His voice in my ear was like sitting by a fire, looking cozily out the window at a cold, splattering rain. The feeling ran in me and over me and through me, and I couldn’t get enough. I just wanted to know who had hurt this man so much and what I could do to help. 

As Harry stood in Frogmore gardens on his drizzly, quintessentially April day, my own feet took me kilometer after kilometer, down a stony-faced Broad Street, past the narrowboats on a leafy Oxford Canal, and into the muddily bucolic Port Meadow. Harry spoke tersely with Willy and Pa, pleading with them to understand why he had fled to America; why the royal life had become unbearable; why he had needed to write this book. And as my feet squelched, then slowly looped back towards home, Harry turned to me, and we started from the very beginning. 

***

We had dinner for the first time the next night. I took my off-beige chicken and potatoes and peas, found a quiet spot down one long table in the College Hall, swallowed back the humiliation of dining alone, and put my Airpods back in. And my world again dissolved into his. He told me about walking behind his mother’s casket, the blinding flashes of the cameras, and the red mist of adolescent fury that washed over his schoolboy years. The next morning, as I walked to the library, he went off to Eton, surrounded by bodyguards, always sticking out, and on my way home, he became very quiet, full of raw, genuine remorse for his unthinking teenage ways. That whole week, I absorbed his pain. I hated his family, I hated the press, and I hated the society that refused to forgive him.

And yet, his voice also made me love England more. We visited beautiful places together: idyllic summer barbecues at Balmoral in Scotland, tea service with the Queen at Buckingham, and cozy evenings at Nottingham Cottage, tucked on the grounds of Kensington Palace. And as Harry laid royalty bare, every door in Oxford opened itself up to me. I could see the gah-dens behind every wall, feel the history made tangible beneath my feet, could practically see the whole country unfolding itself at the horizon. Watching friends laugh together on the street no longer racked me with jealousy; Harry’s companionship was a buoy that stabilized my days. And when I ordered at a coffee shop, I held my head high, because no matter my American accent—I was friends with your prince. I knew his lineage, and I knew just how Granny—that’s the Queen to you—took her tea. I knew England. It finally felt like home. 

***

When Harry met Meghan Markle, I was lying in my hard Danish bed. But as the red mist faded away and his world exploded into a kaleidoscope of joy, I nearly shed a tear. The next morning, the bed was softer, and my world was more colorful too. I was radiant at Monday dinner, regaling Hollie and Laura with royal stories they had never heard. And instead of retreating back into Spare that night, I spent it with them instead, for their company, but also to restrict myself; to savor the few hours Harry and I had left. I limited my listening to half an hour a day, punting the moment when we’d have to part ways. I opted for real dinner company more and more, always looking forward to when Hollie asked, “So what’s going on with Prince Harry today?”

***

When Granny died on a Saturday afternoon, I curled up on my floor, and I really did cry. I cried, because the queen was dead. I cried, because Harry had lost the only royal left who seemed to have a heart. I cried, because he hadn’t gotten to say goodbye. I cried, because there was so much pain left in his voice; all of the unfairness that his family couldn’t grasp, all the British hatred of him and his wife, and the omnipresent blinding flash. I cried, because Meghan’s love for him was so pure and because her companionship really could keep him afloat. I cried, because England was a complex but beautiful place. And mostly I cried because I was going to miss his voice so much.

Thank you, Harry. I hope you’ve been well.
